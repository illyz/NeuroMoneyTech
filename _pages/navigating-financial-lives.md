  <section class="study-page__section" id="study-overview" aria-labelledby="study-overview-title">
    <span class="research-section-label">Study overview</span>
    <h2 id="study-overview-title">Understanding everyday financial lives</h2>

    <p>Money affects almost every part of daily life, but little research has examined how autistic adults experience money management or use financial technology.</p>

    <p>This study involved interviews with 20 autistic adults living in the UK. The interviews used <a href="{{ '/research/collaborative-visual-thinking/' | relative_url }}">Collaborative Visual Thinking</a>, a method in which the participant and researcher create a shared visual artefact during their conversation. The visual artefacts helped participants explore income, spending, saving, tools, routines, and the people involved in financial decisions.</p>

    <p>The study considered both challenges and strengths. It also looked closely at the creative ways participants changed tools, combined technologies, and built personal systems that worked for them.</p>

    <div class="study-page__takeaway" role="note" aria-label="Main finding">
      <strong>Main finding</strong>
      <p>There is no single autistic way of managing money. Participants' needs, preferences, circumstances, and strategies were highly individual.</p>
    </div>
  </section>

  <section class="study-page__section" id="moneywork" aria-labelledby="moneywork-title">
    <span class="research-section-label">Key idea</span>
    <h2 id="moneywork-title">What is moneywork?</h2>

    <div class="study-page__moneywork-layout">
      <div class="study-page__moneywork-copy">
        <p class="study-page__definition"><strong>Moneywork is the work we do to make money work in everyday life.</strong></p>

        <p>Like housework, moneywork is ongoing labour that can be easy to overlook. It requires time, attention, decision-making, and sometimes emotional effort.</p>

        <p>It can include budgeting, paying bills, shopping, earmarking money for different purposes, and learning about finances. It also includes keeping track of digital and analogue systems, as well as emotional and relational work around money.</p>
      </div>

  <section class="study-page__section" id="different-experiences" aria-labelledby="different-experiences-title">
    <span class="research-section-label">What we learned</span>
    <h2 id="different-experiences-title">Autism shaped moneywork in different ways</h2>

    <p>We asked participants how they felt being autistic affected their financial management and wider moneywork. They described analytical skills, attention patterns, a need for control, uncertainty, communication differences, sensory or cognitive overload, and co-occurring conditions.</p>

    <p>These experiences could create difficulties, strengths, or both, depending on the person and situation.</p>

  <section class="study-page__section" id="adaptations" aria-labelledby="adaptations-title">
    <span class="research-section-label">Adaptations and strategies</span>
    <h2 id="adaptations-title">Participants made financial tools work for them</h2>

    <p>Participants rarely relied on one banking app or one standard approach. They combined accounts, spreadsheets, notebooks, reminders, automation, lists, colour coding, and routines into personal systems.</p>

    <p>Some approaches reduced demands: automating a payment could remove the need to remember it. Others deliberately added friction: moving money to a harder-to-access account could help prevent unintended spending.</p>

    <div class="study-page__takeaway" role="note" aria-label="Why these adaptations matter">
      <strong>Why this matters</strong>
      <p>Participants were not simply adapting themselves to financial technology. They were actively adapting technology and their wider financial environment to suit their lives.</p>
    </div>

    <p>For some participants, building these systems was also enjoyable. Analysing data, organising information, colour coding, or pursuing a savings goal could bring a sense of autistic joy, confidence, and control.</p>

  <section class="study-page__section" id="design-implications" aria-labelledby="design-implications-title">
    <span class="research-section-label">Implications for design</span>
    <h2 id="design-implications-title">Inclusive design needs to balance competing needs</h2>

    <p>The findings do not point to one ideal financial interface. A feature that reduces demands for one person may remove useful information or control for someone else. The same person's needs may also change across tasks or over time.</p>

    <figure class="research-figure research-figure--featured">
      <a href="{{ '/assets/images/research/nfl-design-tensions.svg' | relative_url }}" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" aria-label="Open the financial technology design tensions diagram at full size">
        <img src="{{ '/assets/images/research/nfl-design-tensions.svg' | relative_url }}" width="960" height="600" loading="lazy" alt="Diagram showing five competing needs that financial technology design may need to balance. A complete text version follows below.">
      </a>
      <figcaption>
        <span>Five tensions that illustrate why a single design approach cannot work for everyone.</span>
        <a href="{{ '/assets/images/research/nfl-design-tensions.svg' | relative_url }}" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Open full-size image</a>
      </figcaption>
    </figure>

    <details class="research-transcript">
      <summary>Read this figure as text</summary>
      <div class="research-transcript__content study-figure-transcript">
        <ul>
          <li><strong>Minimalism and aesthetics:</strong> reducing visual clutter can support focus, while visual stimulation can make tasks feel approachable and engaging.</li>
          <li><strong>Simplification and depth:</strong> some people want only essential information; others need detail before making a decision.</li>
          <li><strong>Automation and control:</strong> automation can make regular tasks easier, while close involvement can provide reassurance and control.</li>
          <li><strong>Removing and adding friction:</strong> ease can reduce effort, while intentional barriers can help prevent unintended spending.</li>
          <li><strong>Gamification and over-fixation:</strong> goals, lists, and progress can motivate, but can also encourage an unhealthy focus on completing things perfectly.</li>
        </ul>
      </div>
    </details>

    <h3>What financial services can learn</h3>
    <ul class="study-page__recommendations">
      <li>Offer flexible views, including both simplified and detailed information.</li>
      <li>Let people choose which tasks are automated and which remain under their direct control.</li>
      <li>Allow people to add or remove friction depending on what they are trying to achieve.</li>
      <li>Make motivational features optional and adjustable.</li>
      <li>Support personal systems across multiple accounts, tools, and routines rather than assuming one app should do everything.</li>
    </ul>
