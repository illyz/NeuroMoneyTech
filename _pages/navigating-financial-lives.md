## Overview

Financial technologies are often designed with assumptions about how people should manage money. These assumptions may not always align with autistic people's preferences, routines, and ways of organising everyday life. By understanding how autistic adults actually use and adapt financial technologies, we can develop better and more inclusive financial services.

## Summary

### How Autistic Adults Adapt Financial Technologies, Tools and Strategies

Money affects almost every aspect of daily life, yet very little research has explored how autistic adults manage their finances. This study aimed to understand how autistic people experience money management and how they use, adapt, and sometimes reinvent financial tools and technologies to better meet their needs.  

The research involved interviews with 20 autistic adults living in the UK. Participants took part in a collaborative visual mapping activity, where they worked with the researcher to create a visual representation of their financial lives, including income, spending, savings, people involved in financial decisions, and the tools they use.  

The findings showed that autism can influence money management in both positive and challenging ways. Many participants described strengths such as analytical thinking, attention to detail, and a preference for creating systems and routines. These qualities often helped them track spending, budget carefully, research purchases thoroughly, and avoid financial risks.  

At the same time, participants also described challenges. Financial tasks could be overwhelming due to anxiety, sensory overload, uncertainty, executive functioning difficulties, or co-occurring conditions such as ADHD, chronic illness, or mental health difficulties. Some found it difficult to keep track of spending, plan for the future, or engage with financial tasks consistently.  

Rather than relying solely on standard banking tools, participants developed many creative strategies to make money management work for them. These included:

* Using multiple bank accounts or digital “pots” to separate money for different purposes.
* Creating personalised spreadsheets, notebooks, or tracking systems.
* Setting reminders, routines, and automations to reduce memory demands.
* Adding friction to spending by deleting apps or storing money in harder-to-access accounts.
* Using visual systems, colour coding, checklists, and goal tracking to stay motivated.    

An important finding was that many participants did not simply adapt themselves to existing financial technologies; they adapted the technologies to suit their own ways of thinking and working. The study draws on the concept of neuroqueer technoscience, which views autistic people as active creators and innovators rather than passive users of technology.    

The research also highlighted the importance of autistic joy. Some participants enjoyed creating systems, analysing data, colour-coding spreadsheets, or designing personalised financial tools. Activities often viewed as “obsessive” or “rigid” were instead experienced as enjoyable, empowering, and helpful ways of managing finances.  

Based on these findings, the study argues that financial technologies should be more flexible and customisable. Different autistic people have different needs: some want simplicity, while others want detailed information; some prefer automation, while others need a greater sense of control. Inclusive financial technologies should support a range of preferences rather than assuming a single “correct” way to manage money.  

Overall, the study shows that autistic adults bring creativity, resilience, and expertise to managing their financial lives. Rather than focusing only on barriers and difficulties, it highlights how autistic people actively shape financial technologies and practices to fit their needs, and how future financial services could learn from these adaptations.
